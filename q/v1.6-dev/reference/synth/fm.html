FM Synthesis

Overview

FM synthesis makes a spectrum out of sine waves by having one oscillator move another’s phase. Two sines and one number, the modulation index, produce a harmonic series that a filter would need several poles to approximate, and the index can move under an envelope, so the timbre changes through a note the way an acoustic instrument’s does. That is what made the Yamaha DX7 sound unlike anything before it.

Figure 1. One carrier, modulated at its own frequency, as the index rises from zero and falls back. The pale curve is where it started. Nothing is added to the signal: the carrier’s phase is pushed around, and harmonics appear.

How it sounds

Four of the DX7’s factory patches, played by this engine and rendered offline by example/fm_synth, which carries the four patches and reads the motifs from the MIDI files beside it. Each clip is brought to the same peak, the patches' own levels being more than 20 dB apart.

A clean room DX7

This is a re-architecture from first principles, not a reverse engineering: an FM synthesizer written in ideal units, with the DX7’s published laws mapped onto it in one layer. The hardware’s arithmetic is not reproduced and no emulator’s source was read, so what is inherited is the behavior the ear hears and nothing of the implementation. The arithmetic here is floating point, with the envelope in double precision, on the DX7’s own 49,096 Hz envelope clock. Virtual analog models a circuit and leaves out its analog deficiencies such as noise and drift. This is the same move aimed at a digital instrument, its laws kept and its arithmetic left behind: virtual digital.

Reaching that fidelity took measurement, since there was no DX7 here to record. The laws come from the DX7s measurements the Music Synthesizer for Android project published and from Ken Shirriff’s reverse engineering of the chip. Everything they leave out was measured on Dexed, run as a black box: one mechanism at a time on a two operator patch, comparing harmonic amplitudes in decibels, then a whole patch bisected feature by feature, then a motif through both engines with a frame by frame envelope distance to go with the listening. The factory patches land 0.7 to 2.1 dB apart that way, and cleaner at the onsets, where the hardware’s quantization noise is left out by choice.

That matching was done by Claude, Anthropic’s Fable 5.1 coding agent, working offline: it isolated each law, fitted it, and checked the fit against the reference, one mechanism after another, over two days in September 2026. By hand, one parameter at a time through a plugin’s interface, the same work is weeks of it, and that is why it had never been worth doing this way before.

A design built this way costs nothing to extend, and a modern machine has room the DX7’s two chips did not.

  • A voice takes as many operators as its routing needs, up to fm_max_operators.

  • Each operator can be any Oscillator, a saw or a noise source as readily as a sine, with any EnvelopeGenerator behind it.

  • Any routing the expression allows, beyond the 32 Yamaha shipped.

  • Ratios are real numbers instead of a coarse and fine grid.

  • The index is radians, and levels are decibels.

  • The pitch comes from the caller, so a pitch detector or a bend can move a voice sample by sample.

  • A six operator voice costs around 39 to 61 ns a sample, using modern high performance C++, on one Apple Silicon M2 core, so the DX7’s sixteen notes are a small fraction of what fits.

DX Patcher carries the full account: the sources, why the DX7’s tables have the shape they have, the emulations that came before, and what the measurements found.

The two layers

Q’s parts are in two layers. The fm_ layer is a plain FM synthesizer in ideal units: ratios, decibels, seconds, radians. The dx_ layer maps a Yamaha DX7 patch, whose parameters are 0 to 99 counts with laws of their own, onto it. Everything particular to a DX7 lives in that layer and nowhere else: no type below DX Patcher has a field, a constant or a branch that exists because of the DX7, so a voice built without a DX7 patch carries none of its behavior.

A patch parameter is a count, and the law is what the 1983 hardware did with that count: integer arithmetic, table lookups and a fixed clock. An output level of 60 is not 60 percent of anything, and an envelope rate of 50 is neither a time nor a slope. Those shapes are a good part of why a DX7 patch sounds like a DX7. DX Patcher is where they live, and lists them, along with where each one comes from and how it was checked.

Phase modulation, not frequency modulation

What Yamaha shipped, and what Q implements, modulates the carrier’s phase rather than its frequency. The two are equivalent for a sinusoidal modulator, with one practical difference: with phase modulation the modulation index is independent of the modulator’s frequency, so a patch transposes across the keyboard without its brightness drifting. index in FM Algorithm is that deviation, in radians per unit of modulator output.

A carrier at \(f_c\), modulated at \(f_m\) with index \(\beta\), is

\[y(t) = \sin(2 \pi f_c t + \beta \sin(2 \pi f_m t))\]
Figure 2. A 2 kHz carrier modulated at 400 Hz. Every index adds a pair of sidebands at 400 Hz spacing, and the spectrum spreads as the index rises: at 5 radians the sidebands reach past 6 kHz, and the carrier itself is 15 dB down.

Its spectrum is a pair of sidebands at every \(f_c \pm k f_m\), with amplitudes given by the Bessel functions \(J_k(\beta)\). Two consequences worth keeping in mind: the bandwidth grows with the index, and sidebands that fall below zero reflect back into the spectrum with their phase inverted, which is where FM’s inharmonic and metallic sounds come from.

Conformance to the Oscillator concept

An Oscillator is a function object that takes in a phase_iterator, an iterator over the phase, and returns a sample. The phase belongs to the caller, which is what allows any oscillator to stand in for any other.

An FM Operator is one, and so is an FM Voice: both are called with the phase iterator that carries the pitch, and each operator derives its own frequency from it as a ratio. Nothing inside sets the pitch, so a keyboard, a pitch detector or a glide drives a voice alike, and it follows within a sample.

q::phase_iterator master{440_Hz, sps};
auto out = voice(master++);

An operator given a fixed frequency instead of a ratio ignores the iterator, which is how the DX7’s fixed mode works.

Include

#include <q/synth/fm/fm_voice.hpp>      // the voice, and all it needs
#include <q/synth/fm/dx_patcher.hpp>    // to play DX7 patches
#include <q/synth/fm/dx_sysex.hpp>      // to read them from .syx files

Example

A two operator voice, the second modulating the first an octave up:

using namespace q::fm_ops;
constexpr auto silent = q::dx_envelope_gen::floor;

// Two sine operators, the second modulating the first
q::basic_fm_voice<q::fm_operator, q::fm_operator> voice{
{
   .algorithm = {
      op<2> >> op<1>, // operator 2 modulates operator 1
      4 * q::pi,      // index: radians at full modulation
      0.0f            // no feedback
   }},
   sps
};

// The note: operator 1 at the pitch, operator 2 an octave above it
decltype(voice)::note note;

note.op[0] = {
   1.0,     // at the pitch
   {},      // not a fixed frequency
   {
      .rate  = {5_ms, 200_ms, 1_s, 300_ms},
      .level = {0_dB, -6_dB, -12_dB, silent}
   }};

note.op[1] = {
   2.0,     // an octave above
   {},
   {
      .rate  = {5_ms, 150_ms, 1_s, 300_ms},
      .level = {-6_dB, -18_dB, -30_dB, silent}
   }};

voice.attack(note);

q::phase_iterator master{440_Hz, sps};
for (auto& s : buffer)
   s = voice(master++);

voice.release();

A simpler way: The DX7 way, using a patch:

q::dx_patcher patch{cfg, sps};      // the patch, compiled for this rate
q::fm_voice voice{patch};           // six sine operators, set up from it
voice.attack(patch, 69, 0.8f);      // key 69 (A4) at velocity 0.8
