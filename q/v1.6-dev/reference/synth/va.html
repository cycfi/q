Virtual Analog

Overview

Virtual analog is subtractive synthesis: start with a waveform rich in harmonics, take some away with a filter, and shape what is left with envelopes. It is what the polyphonic synthesizers of the late 1970s and 1980s did, and the reason their parts are worth modeling is that the circuits had character, not merely function.

Q’s virtual analog parts are general building blocks that take after those instruments without imitating any of them. Where a behavior comes from a real circuit, the page for it says which and why.

Figure 1. A 200 Hz sawtooth as the filter closes over it, from 12 kHz down to 100 Hz and back. The harmonics were all there to begin with, and the filter takes them away until a sine is left.

How it sounds

Six short phrases through a voice built from these parts: two Analog Oscillator a few cents apart, a ladder filter with an envelope on its cutoff, and the envelopes and LFO around them. One patch per character, voiced by ear. The first five use OTA Ladder Filter. The last is a different animal: Moog Ladder Filter with its saturation on, a square an octave below the two oscillators, and a Soft Clip in front of the filter, which is where the growl of that sound comes from.

example/va_synth renders them all: the patches are written out in its source, and the notes come from the MIDI files beside it, read with `q::midi_file`.

The parts

Component What it does

Analog Oscillator

One oscillator core giving sawtooth, pulse and triangle from a single phase, as a voltage controlled oscillator chip does.

Saw Wave Oscillator, Pulse Wave Oscillator, Triangle Wave Oscillator, Square Wave Oscillator

The individual waveforms, band limited, for a voice built on a single shape.

Hard Sync

One oscillator restarted by another, the sync input that makes a lead tear as it sweeps.

Ladder Filter

The four-pole ladder filter, with the cell and the pole count as parameters.

Moog Ladder Filter

That ladder with a transistor ladder’s cell: odd harmonics, the growl.

OTA Ladder Filter

The same with a variable gain cell, as the Curtis chips in a Prophet-5 or an OB-Xa had: second harmonic, a smoother drive.

State Variable Filter

A state variable filter, for 12 dB per octave and the other responses.

adsr_envelope_gen

The envelopes.

LFO Generator

The modulation source, with delay and fade.

What the era’s circuits contribute

Three behaviors here exist because the hardware behaved that way, and each is a control rather than an assumption:

  • The filter’s cell. A transistor ladder saturates symmetrically and adds odd harmonics. The Curtis CEM3320, the filter in a Prophet-5 Rev 3, an OB-Xa and an OB-8, uses variable gain cells that run off center, and its data sheet puts the passband distortion at 0.1 percent and "predominantly second harmonic". OTA Ladder Filter has drive and asymmetry for exactly that.

  • The pole count. Four poles is the classic 24 dB per octave voice. The OB-Xa put a two-pole setting on the same chip, and that switch is part of how those records sound, so Ladder Filter takes N as a parameter.

  • The triangle’s symmetry. A CEM3340 specifies its triangle at 50 percent with a 45 to 55 percent range. Skewing it walks the wave toward a sawtooth and brings up the even harmonics, which Triangle Wave Oscillator exposes as a control.

None of this is an emulation. Q models no particular instrument, and nothing here is calibrated against one. What it takes from those circuits is the shape of their behavior, which is documented in data sheets anyone can read.

Include

#include <q/synth/va/analog_osc.hpp>   // the oscillator core
#include <q/synth/va/sync.hpp>         // hard sync
#include <q/fx/ladder.hpp>             // both ladder filters

Example

A voice in the idiom: two oscillators a few cents apart, a sawtooth and a pulse, through the OTA ladder with an envelope on the cutoff.

q::analog_osc a{0.4f}, b{0.5f};
q::phase_iterator pa{220_Hz, sps};
q::phase_iterator pb{q::frequency{220 * std::pow(2.0, 7.0 / 1200)}, sps};

q::ota_ladder filter{600_Hz, sps, 0.3f};
filter.drive(1.2f);

for (auto& s : buffer)
{
   auto e = env();                        // a filter envelope
   filter.cutoff(q::frequency{220 + 4000 * e * e}, sps);

   auto mix = 0.6f * a.saw(pa) + 0.4f * b.pulse(pb);
   s = filter(mix * 0.5f) * amp();
   ++pa;
   ++pb;
}
