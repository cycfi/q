BACF Pitch Shifter

This API is new and unstable: it may change in any release, without a deprecation, until it matures.

Overview

The BACF pitch shifter raises or lowers the pitch of a sound as it plays, by a ratio that may change every sample. It works on single notes and chords alike: an instrument, a voice, a synthesizer. A whammy pedal is one use. At a ratio of 1 the output is the input itself, with no latency.

The BACF pitch shifter is a SOLA (Synchronized Overlap-Add) shifter, one of the methods surveyed in Pitch Shifting. The correlation that places its splices is bitstream autocorrelation (BACF), the same that underlies Q’s pitch detectors.

The shifter is implemented by a class named bacf_pitch_shifter.

How It Works

Splicing by the Period

A ring buffer read faster or slower than it is written shifts the pitch, and its read must jump as it catches up with the write or falls behind it (see Pitch Shifting). A crossfade from one read head to a second, a fixed distance away, hides the click but warbles: it blends two parts of the waveform that are out of phase. They line up only when that distance happens to be a whole number of periods of the audio. With the distance set to a whole number of periods, the warble is gone (Figure 1).

Figure 1. One splice on a tone shifted down a fourth

Going up, the read plays a cycle twice; going down, it skips one; in between, it reads each cycle faster or slower (Figure 2). Pitch shifting is whole periods added or taken away, and the rest resampled.

Figure 2. Each cycle painted by the input period it is read from
Figure 3. Splices going up and going down, on a wave with a period of 5 samples

Figure 3 shows the two kinds of splice, on a delay line holding a wave that repeats every 5 samples:

  • Pitch up: the read moves faster than the write and catches up with it. Before it reaches the newest sample, it jumps back one period, to the same point in the cycle, and carries on from there. Each jump replays a period, which is how a sound gets higher without getting shorter. The read stays only as far behind the write as the next crossfade needs, since a crossfade going up gains on the write.

  • Pitch down: the read moves slower than the write and falls behind. Once it is a whole period behind, it jumps forward, by as many periods as fit, to just behind the newest sample. Each jump skips periods, which is how a sound gets lower without getting longer. The crossfade then reads the newest samples, and falls behind them, so it never runs out.

The splice forward to just behind the input, going down, comes from Katja Vetter’s [solad~] (Synchronized Overlap Add Delayline), a SOLA pitch shifter for Pure Data. Her articles describe it: Pitch Shifting and Low-Latency Pitch Shifting.

Finding the Period

Where the waveform repeats is found by autocorrelation, here by the BACF Lag Tracker. Bitstream autocorrelation, BACF, is a 1-bit autocorrelator: each sample becomes a single bit, 1 above zero and 0 below (see Bitstream Autocorrelation). The video below shows the mechanism. The latest bits (blue) are laid over the bits from a moment earlier (orange), and every bit where they differ is marked (red). As the lag slides back, the red shows how much the two differ: less red, closer match.

The green curve plots the share of bits that differ at each lag. Where the waveform best matches itself, the two line up and the red almost vanishes. That lag is where the waveform repeats. Here, an E chord lines up at 24.3 ms, its common period.

The read heads are spaced by that lag, updated continuously as the music changes, so they stay in step with the waveform’s own period. For a single note, the distance is the note’s period, or a multiple of it; any multiple splices as well, so octave errors do no harm. For a chord, it is the chord’s common period, which for the open E chord in the video is twice the period of the low E. In that time, the six strings complete 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 8 cycles of E2, B2, E3, G#3, B3 and E4. Periods from 2.5 to 45 ms are found: enough for the lowest guitar note (12 ms), for the common period of an open E chord (24 ms), and for an open G (41 ms): its G, B and D stand at about 4:5:6, so the chord repeats only every four periods of its low G.

The period is tracked continuously, one bitstream comparison at each zero crossing of the signal through a 1 kHz low pass, so a splice needs no search, only a refine: the jump moves by up to 4 samples, to where the full signal matches best. The tracked period is smoothed as Q’s period detectors smooth theirs: a new lock, or a jump to another period, is taken at once; anything else passes through a median of 3, which keeps a one-off misreading out of the splices, and an average.

Overlap-Add

BACF and the two read heads make the synchronized part of SOLA. The overlap-add part is the smooth crossfade between the two read heads (Figure 4). It hides any small differences left between the two segments spliced together. Each splice crossfades over half a period, between 2 and 8 ms. No jump is shorter than 1.25 crossfades, so a very short period is spliced two or more periods at a time.

Figure 4. A splice: the read now, fading out, and the read shifted back by one period, fading in

Attacks

The attack of a note is not periodic: the burst at its start, such as a pick leaving a string, a stick striking or a bow catching, happens once. A splice through it would repeat or cut it. So at each attack, found by a Transient Detector, the shifter starts the read again at the attack’s own start, a millisecond before the detection:

  • Going down, at once. The read only falls behind, so the attack plays from its start, slowed.

  • Going up, as soon as the new note’s period is found, and after the maximum latency (20 ms by default) at the latest. Until then, the previous note keeps playing.

So going up, an attack comes out up to the maximum latency late. That is the latency the shifter reports (see Latency).

At an attack the shifter also drops the old period and looks for the new note’s, on material from after the attack only: a new note has a new period, and splicing it by the old one would mismatch. With only material after the last attack tracked, a splice back never repeats the attack (a flam), and a splice forward never jumps over it.

At Rest

At a ratio of exactly 1, the next attack, or silence, returns the read to the input itself: from then on the output is the input, sample for sample, with no latency. Bending away from rest starts from there with no seam. A pull-up splices back from the newest sample; a dive simply lets the read fall behind.

Formants

SOLA reads the audio faster or slower by the pitch ratio, which moves the formants, the fixed resonances that color a sound, along with the pitch. Shifted up far enough, a sound takes on the chipmunk effect. Formant Preservation restores the formants after the shifter (Figure 5).

Figure 5. The BACF pitch shifter with formant correction

Latency

  • At rest: none. The output is the input itself.

  • Going down: an attack comes out a millisecond behind the input, the pre-roll. Between attacks the read falls behind by at most a period and a crossfade, before a splice forward brings it back to the input: on average 2 to 11 ms behind on single notes and lines of notes, and 14 to 20 ms on chords, which splice on the period their notes have in common.

  • Going up: between attacks the read stays the next crossfade’s headroom and up to a period behind: on average 2 to 8 ms on single notes and lines, 12 to 16 ms on chords. An attack in a line of notes comes out about 6 to 10 ms late; a note out of silence, with no period to go on, at most latency() late: the maximum latency (20 ms by default) and the millisecond of pre-roll.

  • Wide chords going up: a chord that repeats only every 41 ms, such as an open G chord on a guitar, cannot be matched until that much of it, and the match window, has been heard. Its attack still comes out on time, at its own pitch, and is shifted from about 50 ms on.

Input

The shifter does best on a clean signal, direct from the source: for a guitar, straight from the instrument. Put it first in the chain, and any amplification, distortion or effects after it. Noise and processing get in the way of the matches it splices on:

  • Noise and hum. As a note decays, a noise floor or mains hum close to its level spoils the match, and the splices land on mismatched material: a warble in the tail.

  • Distortion and heavy processing. Distortion keeps the highs flickering the way an attack does, so the transient detector finds attacks that are not there, and each one restarts the read (see Attacks): a warble through the sustain.

  • A microphone. It adds the room’s noise, and on a miked amplifier the amplifier’s own noise and break-up on a loud chord.

Example

An octave-up whammy pedal: an expression pedal goes from the heel, the dry input, to the toe, an octave up. The pedal is read once a block and smoothed, so the bend glides between readings:

#include <q/fx/bacf_pitch_shifter.hpp>
#include <q/fx/lowpass.hpp>             // For one_pole_lowpass
#include <q/support/literals.hpp>
#include <cmath>
#include <span>
...
namespace q = cycfi::q;
using namespace q::literals;

float sps;                       // samples per second
std::span<float> in;             // the input samples
float pedal;                     // the pedal, 0 (heel) to 1 (toe), read once a block
...
auto shift = q::bacf_pitch_shifter{sps};                     (1)
auto smooth = q::one_pole_lowpass{100_Hz, sps};              (2)
...
for (auto& s : in)
{
   auto const semitones = smooth(12.0f * pedal);             (3)
   s = shift(s, std::exp2(semitones / 12.0f));               (4)
}
1 Construct the shifter, with the default 20 ms maximum latency.
2 A low pass to smooth the pedal, so the bend has no steps.
3 The pedal in semitones: 0 at the heel, 12 at the toe.
4 Shift by the ratio for those semitones. Back at the heel, the smoothed value settles to a ratio of exactly 1 within about 30 ms, and the shifter passes the input through.

See example/whammy in the Q repository for a complete program: it plays guitar recordings through the shifter, driven by the pitch bend of an e-Whammy recorded as MIDI.

Implementation Details

Implementation details are subject to change without notice. Rely only on the expressions above.

Transient Detection

The Transient Detector finds the attacks by listening above 4 kHz, where an attack puts a burst of energy and a sustained note keeps little. A distorted signal keeps that band busy all the time, which is why distortion before the shifter makes it find false picks.

Performance

Measured at 48 kHz in blocks of 32 samples (0.67 ms), on an Apple M2 and on an STM32H723 at 550 MHz with caches on. Average: the time a sample, and its share of a sample period. Worst: the slowest block, and its share of the block’s 0.67 ms; over 100%, it runs late. Figures are rounded and vary with the build and the input.

Measured shifting an E chord by -12, -5 and +12 semitones, with the default 20 ms maximum latency:

Apple M2 STM32H723

A sample, on average

14 to 17 ns (0.07 to 0.08%)

0.71 to 0.84 µs (3.4 to 4%)

Worst block of 32 samples

13 to 40 µs (2 to 6%)

96 to 102 µs (14 to 15%)

The worst blocks come at a splice: its refine compares the two sides of the jump at 9 offsets, about 4,000 multiply-adds at once. On the M2 they are mostly the operating system’s scheduling, not the shifter. The shifter allocates about 56 KB at 48 kHz, most of it the 45 ms history.
