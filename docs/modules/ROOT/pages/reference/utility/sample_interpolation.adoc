= Sample Interpolation

include::../../common.adoc[]

== Overview

The `sample_interpolation` namespace provides a family of interpolation policies for fractional (sub-sample) buffer indexing. Given a buffer and a fractional index, an interpolation policy computes a value between samples. The policies are used by the `{mono-fractional_ring_buffer}`, and consequently by the fractional delays built on top of it, where delay times are not integer multiples of the sampling period, most importantly delay lines whose tap positions are modulated, as in chorus, flanger, vibrato, and pitch shifting effects.

The index convention follows the `{ring_buffer}`: index 0 refers to the newest element and increasing indices go back in time.

Six policies are provided, trading accuracy, smoothness, and computational cost:

[cols="1,1,1,1,1", options="header"]
|===
| Type      | Points | Passes through samples | Reproduces exactly  | Continuity

| `none`    | 1      | Yes                    | n/a                 | Discontinuous
| `linear`  | 2      | Yes                    | Constants, ramps    | C^0^
| `cosine`  | 2      | Yes                    | Constants           | C^1^
| `cubic`   | 4      | Yes                    | Up to cubics        | C^0^
| `hermite` | 4      | Yes                    | Up to quadratics    | C^1^
| `bspline` | 4      | No (smoother)          | Constants, ramps    | C^2^
|===

The arithmetic of each policy is also available as a free function, an interpolation primitive, for samples that are not in a buffer. Two related functions locate features between samples: `peak_offset` finds the vertex of a sampled maximum, and `zero_projection` finds where the tangent at a point crosses zero.

== The Interpolation Types

The figures below all interpolate the same set of samples.

=== `none`

image::no_interpolation.svg[alt="No Interpolation", width=700px, align=center, title="No Interpolation", link={imagesdir}/no_interpolation.svg]

The fractional part of the index is truncated and the sample at the integral index is returned, holding each value until the next: a zero-order hold. It costs no arithmetic, and its staircase gives a strong wideband error (~`w/2` worst-case on a sinusoid with angular increment `w`). Use it when indices are integral, or where speed comes first.

=== `linear`

image::linear_interpolation.svg[alt="Linear Interpolation", width=700px, align=center, title="Linear Interpolation", link={imagesdir}/linear_interpolation.svg]

First-order interpolation between the two bracketing samples. It reproduces linear ramps exactly, and its worst-case error on a sinusoid is ~`w²/8`, for one multiply. It is the cheapest type that passes through the samples, and the default.

=== `cosine`

image::cosine_interpolation.svg[alt="Cosine Interpolation", width=700px, align=center, title="Cosine Interpolation", link={imagesdir}/cosine_interpolation.svg]

A 2-point cosine ease with the footprint of `linear` and a continuous first derivative: the slope is zero at every sample, where the curve in the figure flattens. The output never overshoots: it stays within `[y1, y2]`. The ease is exact only at fractional positions 0, 0.5, and 1, so on band-limited audio it is less accurate than `linear`.

Use it to smooth control signals (automation, envelope breakpoints, morph positions, value noise), where pass-through, C^1^ continuity, and bounded output matter more than spectral accuracy. The ease, `sin²(πμ/2)`, comes from the q sin lookup table, with no trigonometric call.

=== `cubic`

image::cubic_interpolation.svg[alt="Cubic Interpolation", width=700px, align=center, title="Cubic Interpolation", link={imagesdir}/cubic_interpolation.svg]

4-point, third-order Lagrange interpolation. It passes through the samples and reproduces cubic polynomials exactly: the most accurate of the policies on smooth signals. Its first derivative is discontinuous at the samples, so it suits taps that do not move; for modulated taps, use `hermite`.

=== `hermite`

image::hermite_interpolation.svg[alt="Hermite Interpolation", width=700px, align=center, title="Hermite Interpolation", link={imagesdir}/hermite_interpolation.svg]

4-point cubic Hermite with Catmull-Rom tangents (central differences). It passes through the samples, reproduces quadratics exactly, and is C^1^-continuous across segment boundaries, so a tap sweeping through the buffer meets no derivative discontinuities. It is the standard choice for modulated delay-line taps in chorus, flanger, vibrato, and pitch shifting effects.

=== `bspline`

image::bspline_interpolation.svg[alt="B-spline Interpolation", width=700px, align=center, title="B-spline Interpolation", link={imagesdir}/bspline_interpolation.svg]

4-point cubic B-spline, a smoother. It does not pass through the samples (at an integer index it yields `(y[i-1] + 4·y[i] + y[i+1])/6`), and has the best high-frequency rejection and smoothness (C^2^) of the cubics. Constants and ramps are reproduced exactly. Use it when the buffer holds noisy data.

=== Accuracy and Cost

image::interpolation_quality.svg[alt="Interpolation Quality", width=700px, align=center, title="Worst-case error reading a 1 kHz sinusoid (48 kHz sampling) at fractional offsets", link={imagesdir}/interpolation_quality.svg]

On band-limited content, the 4-point types are two to three orders of magnitude more accurate than `linear`. `cosine` is less accurate than `linear`, and `bspline` attenuates in-band content.

image::interpolation_cpu.svg[alt="Interpolation CPU Cost", width=700px, align=center, title="Measured cost per interpolated read: 1024-sample buffer, random fractional indices, Apple M2, clang -O3", link={imagesdir}/interpolation_cpu.svg]

[cols="1,1,1,1", options="header"]
|===
| Type      | Samples read | Arithmetic (approx.)   | Special functions

| `none`    | 1            | n/a                    | n/a
| `linear`  | 2            | 1 ×, 3 ±               | n/a
| `cosine`  | 2            | ~5 ×, 5 ±              | sin lookup table (~1 KiB)
| `cubic`   | 4            | 9 ×, 10 ±              | n/a
| `hermite` | 4            | 9 ×, 9 ±               | n/a
| `bspline` | 4            | 10 ×, 10 ±             | n/a
|===

All types are stateless and branch-free. The 4-point types cost roughly 2 to 3 times `linear`, and `hermite` is the fastest of them. The sin lookup table behind `cosine` stores the first quarter cycle, about 1 KiB of read-only memory. The measurements come from `test/benchmark/interpolation_bench.cpp`.

=== Expressions

==== Notation

`X`         :: Any `sample_interpolation` type, e.g. `sample_interpolation::hermite`.
`x`         :: Object of type `X`.
`buf`       :: A buffer indexable with `std::size_t`, where index 0 refers to the newest element, e.g. `ring_buffer<float>`.
`i`         :: Fractional index. Object of an arithmetic type `I`, e.g. `float`.

==== Function Call

[cols="1,1,1"]
|===
| Expression      | Semantics                                  | Return Type

| `x(buf, i)`     | Get the value of `buf` interpolated at
                    fractional index `i`. `none` truncates
                    the fractional part.                       | `I`
|===

The valid fractional index range depends on how many samples the type reads. The 4-point types read one sample on each side of the bracketing pair:

[cols="1,1", options="header"]
|===
| Types                                     | Valid fractional index range

| `none`                                    | `[0, size-1]`
| `linear`, `cosine`                        | `[0, size-2]`
| `cubic`, `hermite`, `bspline`             | `[1, size-3]`
|===

WARNING: An index outside the valid range reads outside the buffer. On a `{ring_buffer}` the index wraps around and yields unrelated samples; on other buffers it is undefined behavior.

NOTE: All policies are stateless: interpolation is a pure function of the buffer contents and the index. The `{mono-fractional_ring_buffer}` invokes the policy from its index operator and converts the result to its `value_type`. A policy is selected by its `Interpolation` template parameter:

```c++
using hermite_buffer = fractional_ring_buffer<
   float, std::vector<float>, float, sample_interpolation::hermite>;
```

==== Example

Read the history of the input samples `in` 220.5 samples back, with `hermite` interpolation:

```c++
#include <q/utility/ring_buffer.hpp>  // For ring_buffer

namespace q = cycfi::q;

auto hist = q::ring_buffer<float>{1024};
auto hermite = q::sample_interpolation::hermite{};        <1>

for (auto& s : in)
{
   hist.push(s);
   s = hermite(hist, 220.5f);                             <2>
}
```

<1> Construct the `hermite` policy. It holds no state.
<2> Get the value between `hist[220]` and `hist[221]`, from the four samples
    `hist[219]` to `hist[222]`.

== Interpolation Primitives

The interpolation primitives are the arithmetic of the policies, as `constexpr` function templates, for samples already in hand: a window function evaluated between table entries, a control value eased between breakpoints, samples read from a history being walked. `mu` runs from 0 at `y1` to 1 at `y2`. The 4-point forms take one sample on each side of that pair, `y0` before `y1` and `y3` after `y2`.

`lagrange6_interpolate` is the fifth-order Lagrange form over six points, `y0` to `y5`, with `mu` running from 0 at `y2` to 1 at `y3`. It passes through the samples and is exact on quintics. It reads six samples where the 4-point forms read four, and stays accurate on coarsely sampled signals: at 5.6 samples per period, its worst error on a sinusoid is about a quarter of that of `hermite`. It has no policy type.

image::lagrange6_accuracy.svg[alt="Worst-case error of cubic, hermite and lagrange6 interpolation", width=700px, align=center, title="The worst-case error reading a unit sinusoid between samples, against how many samples each period has. The six-point form gains accuracy faster as the sampling gets finer, and is about four times better at 5.6 samples per period.", link={imagesdir}/lagrange6_accuracy.svg]

=== Include

```c++
#include <q/utility/interpolation_primitives.hpp>
#include <q/utility/interpolation.hpp>              // cosine_interpolate
```

`interpolation_primitives.hpp` includes nothing else. `cosine_interpolate` needs the sin lookup table, so it is in `interpolation.hpp`, which includes `interpolation_primitives.hpp`.

=== Declaration

```c++
template <typename T>
constexpr T    linear_interpolate(T y1, T y2, T mu);

template <typename T>

                , T y1
                , T y2
                , T y3
                , T y4
                , T y5

               );
```

=== Expressions

==== Notation

`T`                 :: Floating point type, e.g. `float`.
`y0` ... `y5`       :: Objects of type `T`: equally spaced samples, in order.
`mu`                :: Object of type `T` from 0.0 to 1.0: the position between `y1` and `y2` (between `y2` and `y3` for `lagrange6_interpolate`).

==== Functions

[cols="1,1,1"]
|===
| Expression                                            | Semantics                                 | Return Type

| `linear_interpolate(y1, y2, mu)`                      | Interpolate linearly between `y1`
                                                          and `y2` at `mu`.                         | `T`
| `cosine_interpolate(y1, y2, mu)`                      | Interpolate between `y1` and `y2` at
                                                          `mu` with a cosine ease.                  | `T`
| `cubic_interpolate(y0, y1, y2, y3, mu)`               | Interpolate between `y1` and `y2` at
                                                          `mu` by third-order Lagrange.             | `T`
| `hermite_interpolate(y0, y1, y2, y3, mu)`             | Interpolate between `y1` and `y2` at
                                                          `mu` by cubic Hermite (Catmull-Rom).      | `T`
| `bspline_interpolate(y0, y1, y2, y3, mu)`             | Smooth between `y1` and `y2` at `mu`
                                                          by cubic B-spline.                        | `T`
| `lagrange6_interpolate(y0, y1, y2, y3, y4, y5, mu)`   | Interpolate between `y2` and `y3` at
                                                          `mu` by fifth-order Lagrange.             | `T`
|===

Each primitive has the properties of its policy, described in <<_the_interpolation_types>>.

==== Example

Read a table at a fractional position:

```c++
#include <q/utility/interpolation_primitives.hpp>
#include <span>
...
namespace q = cycfi::q;

std::span<float const> table;       // the table
float pos;                          // the position
...
auto i = std::size_t(pos);
auto mu = pos - i;
auto y = q::hermite_interpolate(
   table[i - 1], table[i], table[i + 1], table[i + 2], mu);     <1>
```

<1> Interpolate between `table[i]` and `table[i + 1]`, from the four entries
    around `pos`. `pos` must be at least 1, and at most the size of the
    table minus 3.

== Peak Offset

`peak_offset` locates a discrete maximum between samples: a correlation peak, a waveform apex, a spectral bin. Given three equally spaced samples, it returns the vertex of the parabola through them, as an offset from the middle sample. {best_lag} uses it to refine the lag it finds by exhaustive search.

image::peak_offset.svg[alt="Peak Offset", width=700px, align=center, title="The peak of a sampled lobe falls between samples. `y1` is the largest sample, but not the peak; the parabola through its neighbors recovers the remaining third of a sample.", link={imagesdir}/peak_offset.svg]

The fit assumes the shape is symmetric about its peak. On an asymmetric lobe the bias belongs to the shape, and cancels in the difference between two positions on the same feature. The fit measures the distance between two peaks more accurately than it places one peak in absolute time.

=== Include

```c++
#include <q/utility/interpolation_primitives.hpp>
```

=== Declaration

```c++
template <typename T>
constexpr T    peak_offset(T y0, T y1, T y2);
```

=== Expressions

==== Notation

`T`                 :: Floating point type, e.g. `float`.
`y0`, `y1`, `y2`    :: Objects of type `T`: three equally spaced samples, in order.

==== Functions

[cols="1,1,1"]
|===
| Expression                  | Semantics                                 | Return Type

| `peak_offset(y0, y1, y2)`   | Get the offset, in samples, from `y1` to
                                the vertex of the parabola through `y0`,
                                `y1` and `y2`, positive toward `y2`.      | `T`
|===

For a triple that brackets a maximum (`y1` no smaller than `y0` and `y2`), the offset is within `[-0.5, 0.5]`. `peak_offset` returns 0 when `y0 - 2 * y1 + y2` is not negative (the triple is not concave). For a minimum, negate the inputs.

WARNING: Pass a triple that brackets a maximum. A concave triple that does not, such as `(0, 2, 3)`, gives the vertex of the parabola beyond the samples: an offset of 1.5.

==== Example

Refine the position of the largest sample of a lobe in a block held in time order:

```c++
#include <q/utility/interpolation_primitives.hpp>
#include <span>
...
namespace q = cycfi::q;

std::span<float const> buf;         // the block, in time order
std::size_t i;                      // the index of the largest sample
...
float pos = i + q::peak_offset(buf[i - 1], buf[i], buf[i + 1]);  <1>
```

<1> Add the offset of the vertex from `buf[i]` to the index of the largest
    sample.

== Zero Projection

`zero_projection` takes a point on a flank, its value `y` and its slope `dy` per sample, and returns the signed offset in samples from that point to where its tangent crosses zero. For a point at position `x`, the crossing is at `x + zero_projection(y, dy)`.

The projection depends on the point alone. A slow shoulder that moves the waveform's zero crossing away from a pulse does not enter it, and a pure amplitude decay scales `y` and `dy` together, so the projection stays fixed as a note dies. Taken at the steepest sample of a flank, found with `peak_offset` over the successive differences, it gives a timing mark that is level referenced like a zero crossing and resists noise like a steep edge.

image::zero_projection.svg[alt="The tangent at the steepest sample projecting to the pulse edge", width=700px, align=center, title="A raised-cosine pulse on a slow ramp. The waveform itself crosses zero at 0, where the ramp does. The tangent at the steepest sample of the rising flank crosses zero at 24.6, next to the left edge of the pulse, at 24.", link={imagesdir}/zero_projection.svg]

```c++

```

=== Declaration

```c++

```

=== Expressions

==== Notation

`T`         :: Floating point type, e.g. `float`.
`y`         :: Object of type `T`: the value at the point.
`dy`        :: Object of type `T`: the slope at the point, per sample.

==== Functions

[cols="1,1,1"]
|===
| Expression                | Semantics                                 | Return Type

| `zero_projection(y, dy)`  | Get the signed offset, in samples, from
                              the point to where its tangent crosses
                              zero. Returns 0 when `dy` is 0.           | `T`
|===

==== Example

Mark the time of a rising edge to a fraction of a sample, in a block held in time order:

```c++
#include <q/utility/interpolation_primitives.hpp>
#include <span>
...
namespace q = cycfi::q;

std::span<float const> buf;         // the block, in time order
std::size_t i;                      // the index of the steepest sample of the flank
...
float dy = (buf[i + 1] - buf[i - 1]) / 2;                 <1>
float edge = i + q::zero_projection(buf[i], dy);          <2>
```

<1> Estimate the slope at `i` by the central difference.
<2> Add the offset to the tangent's zero crossing to the index of the point.
